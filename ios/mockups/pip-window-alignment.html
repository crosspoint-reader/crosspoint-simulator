What is actually in the way

iOS PiP is a system window in another process. Two consequences drive everything below:

1. The app cannot see it. There is no public API that reports another app's PiP window — its presence, its corner, or its size. AVPictureInPictureController only governs PiP that this app starts. So the pad cannot dodge at runtime; any answer has to be a static layout that survives every corner, or a preference the reader sets by hand.
2. Touches over it never arrive. The PiP window is above the app, so an overlapped control is dead, not merely hidden. The invisible hit-slop already earmarked as the fallback for the short bottom row (ios/README.md) cannot rescue this — slop under a system window is still under a system window.

The budget

Every option here is really a question about one number. The panel presents at an integer scale (CROSSPOINT_SIM_PIXEL_EXACT), so the space below it can grow only until the panel would drop from 2× to 1× — at which point the page halves. That cliff is the whole design constraint.

Two axes

Vertical alignment puts the pad in a band above the PiP: they share a horizontal edge, and the pad is safe in both bottom corners because it is in neither. It is paid for out of the budget above. Horizontal alignment keeps the pad on the screen's bottom edge and vacates one corner: they share a vertical gutter, it costs no vertical budget at all, and it defends exactly one corner.

Trim, whichever structure wins

The PiP window is drawn by the system and cannot be restyled, but the pad can be trimmed to agree with it so the two read as one composition rather than a collision:

  • Gutters. PiP insets ~10 pt from the screen edge; the pad's margin is 20 pt. Taking the pad to 10 pt lines the outer edges up; keeping 20 pt reads as a deliberate inset. Anything between reads as a mistake.
  • Corner radius. The pad's capsules are 12 pt; the PiP window is visibly rounder (~14–16 pt). Matching it costs one constant in paintPad.
  • The floor. PiP sits ~11 pt above the home-indicator inset; the pad sits on it. Dropping the pad's bottom row to the same 45 pt floor puts both on one baseline.
  • The stash. A flick off the left or right edge parks PiP as a tab — the reader's own zero-code escape hatch, worth knowing about whatever ships.

Where this lands

V1 (Lift) is the smallest honest fix — same seven controls, same grid, same fused pairs, moved up as a block until the pad's bottom edge meets the PiP's top edge. It is corner-agnostic, and the only thing it spends is the chassis-matched panel gap, which drops from 14.8% of panel height to roughly a third of that. That ruling is three days old, so it is worth a second look rather than a quiet override.

V2 (One row) is the better shape if the two-row grammar is negotiable. It is the only option where every target clears the 44 pt HIG minimum — the current half-height bottom row does not — and on the Air it reserves a full PiP band while keeping the chassis gap intact. It costs the two-row chassis resemblance and puts POWER back in the same row as BACK.

H1/H2 cost nothing and defend one corner. On a 420 pt screen the leftover strip beside a medium PiP is about 190 pt — the bottom row fits by a pt or two, and stops fitting the moment the reader pinches the window bigger. Fine as a stopgap, fragile as a spec.

Method
  • Frames are computed, not drawn: the same arithmetic as layoutPad() in ios/CrossPointIOSShim.cpp and the manual-fit block in src/HalDisplay.cpp, in points, per device.
  • iPhone Air's top safe-area inset is published as 68 pt; the shipped render measures 74 pt. This sheet uses 74, which reproduces the screenshot's pad rows to the pixel (top row at 2057 px, bottom row at 2539 px).
  • PiP geometry is measured off the screenshot — 198.0 × 111.3 pt (16:9), 10.3 pt from the right edge, 45.0 pt from the bottom. Other sizes are modelled by scaling the width; the slider spans the range iOS offers by pinch.
  • Device point sizes and safe areas: Use Your Loaf. PiP sizing and corner behaviour: Apple Support. Thumb-zone accuracy: A List Apart, Smashing Magazine.
